Partridge Puzzle (n=9, 45x45 grid) - Results
=============================================

Exhaustive search completed: 2026-09-25

The full search of the 45x45 Partridge Puzzle (one 1x1 tile, two 2x2, ... up to
nine 9x9, totaling 45x45=2025 area) is complete: every unit of the search space
has been searched, not just estimated via extrapolation.

Total unique solutions: 1,730,280
  Exact match to Matt Parker's cited figure (see notes.txt and the source
  video, https://youtu.be/eqyuQZHfNPQ, "The impossible puzzle with over a
  million solutions!") - not an approximation, the same number digit for
  digit.

n=8 sub-puzzle (36x36 grid, tiles sized 1-8): 18,656 solutions
  Confirmed two independent ways: reproduced from scratch with our own
  solver, and matches Matthew Scroggs' companion blog post
  (mscroggs.co.uk/blog/119) exactly. The original August 2026 note in
  notes.txt recording "18177" was a transcription error - the real count is
  18,656 (2,332 unique once rotations/reflections are treated as the same
  solution, matching the historical Bill Cutler/Patrick Hamlyn result).
  The from-scratch confirmation run took 15h36m (real time, nsolver.cpp's
  original unoptimized -O2 build, no bitboards, single-threaded) - a
  striking contrast against the full n=9 search this project completed in
  under a week across a multi-worker distributed system with bitboards,
  -O3 -march=native, and remote workers.


How long did the full n=9 search actually take?
--------------------------------------------------
An "apples to apples" answer is tricky, since the project spanned months
with long idle stretches, not continuous effort. Three framings, all
measured to the same completion moment (2026-09-25 19:24:11 UTC, when the
last range finished):

  A) Idea to completion - from the earliest file of the original Python
     solver (solver/, 2026-07-11): 75.78 days.
  B) First solution ever found on disk (CppSolver/Solutions/soln_001_...,
     2026-07-12) to completion: 75.31 days.
  C) Start of the distributed-search rewrite - Claude/rangesolver.cpp's
     first commit (2026-09-20), the range-bounded/bitboard-optimized
     solver that eventually ran across multiple machines - to completion:
     4.87 days.

A and B are raw calendar time, not active compute time - there's no
record of how much of those ~75 days had anything actually running. C is
the fairest comparison against the n=8 confirmation run's 15h36m above,
since both measure "how long did active, continuous searching take" - and
even there, going from 15h36m (single-threaded, unoptimized, n=8) to 4.87
days (distributed across multiple machines, bitboards, -O3 -march=native,
n=9) reflects how much bigger the n=9 search space actually is, not a
regression - n=9 has about 92.7 times as many solutions as n=8, on a board
with 2025 cells instead of 1296, searched 9 tile sizes deep instead of 8.


How fast did each version of the solver actually run?
----------------------------------------------------------
Measured directly (2026-10-02) rather than estimated: each version
instrumented with a node counter and run for a fixed 30-second window from
a cold start (same machine, same full/unrestricted search), reporting raw
search nodes explored per second - a fairer throughput metric than
solutions/sec, since how many solutions turn up in a short window depends
on combinatorial luck about where in the tree you happen to be, not on
raw execution speed.

  Python (solver/main.py, the original):        6,568 nodes/sec
  Original C++ port (CppSolver/solver.cpp,
    -O2, naive 2D array):                   1,050,340 nodes/sec  (160x)
  First Claude/rangesolver.cpp commit
    (naive 2D array, -O2):                  4,658,986 nodes/sec  (4.4x)
  Final rangesolver.cpp (bitboard rows,
    -O3 -march=native):                    13,675,063 nodes/sec  (2.94x)

(the "x" figures are each step's speedup over the row above it)

Notes on what's actually being compared:
  - The Python run found zero solutions in its 30-second window either
    way (it's that much slower), so nodes/sec - not solutions/sec - is
    the only metric that was measurable at all there.
  - solver.cpp -> first rangesolver.cpp is a real, measured 4.4x jump
    despite both using -O2 and a naive (non-bitboard) occupancy
    representation - likely because rangesolver.cpp doesn't maintain the
    extra per-cell size/offset bookkeeping solver.cpp's Grid class keeps
    on every placement (needed for its own rendering approach, not for
    the search itself) - flagged as the probable cause, not fully traced.
  - End to end, original C++ port -> current distributed-solver binary:
    13.02x, combining both the algorithmic change (bitboards) and the
    build-flag change (-O2 -> -O3 -march=native).


L / I / Other classification
-----------------------------
Every solution can be classified by where its nine 9x9 tiles land:

  L:      74,624  (4.313%)
    The nine 9x9 tiles form a forced border along two adjacent edges of the
    board (five tiles along one full 45-length edge, four along the
    remaining 36-length edge), with the 36x36 interior holding exactly one
    n=8 sub-solution and nothing else. Fully determined by the n=8 count:
    exactly 4 x 18,656 (one for each choice of corner), with zero remaining
    freedom once a corner is picked - the border tiling is forced.

  I:   1,303,584  (75.339%)
    The nine 9x9 tiles include a straight line of five, spanning the full
    width or height of the board - not necessarily touching an edge. Splits
    into 1,299,744 where that line is flush against a border, and 3,840
    where it runs through the interior instead (e.g. a horizontal line of
    five 9x9 tiles at some row in the middle of the board).

  Other: 352,072  (20.348%)
    No such line exists among the nine 9x9 tiles at all - they're
    positioned without forming a clean L or I shape.

Note on Matt Parker's video: it states the same three numbers, but the "L"
and "other" labels are swapped relative to what the geometry it describes
actually produces (352,072 is stated as "L", 74,624 as "other" - the
reverse). Also, the video's description of "I" ("a single column of
9x9s... not clear if a column in the interior would also count") is
resolved: yes, an interior line counts, not just one touching an edge -
confirmed directly by finding exactly 3,840 solutions with an interior
line, which is precisely the gap between our edge-only count and Parker's
stated I total.


Structural finding: interior-I lines occur at only one location
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Among the 3,840 "interior I" solutions (a straight line of five 9x9 tiles
running through the middle of the board rather than flush against an edge -
see above), the line's position isn't spread across the 35 possible interior
rows/columns at all. It only ever occurs at a single location, up to the
board's own rotations and reflections: row 16 (equivalently row 20, its
mirror image) for a horizontal line, or column 16/20 for a vertical one -
960 solutions at each of these 4 equivalent positions, 3,840 total, with
zero solutions at any other interior row or column. In every one of these
960-solution groups, the remaining four 9x9 tiles (the ones not part of the
line itself) split exactly 2-and-2 between the two strips the line divides
the board into - never any other split. Why exactly this position (and not,
say, row 14 or 22) is forced isn't nailed down - not chased further for
now - but the search being exhaustive means this is a confirmed fact about
the puzzle, not a pattern noticed in a sample.

The 2-and-2 split itself recurses in a self-similar way. Looking at the
row-16 case specifically (960 solutions): the line divides the board into a
16-tall strip above and a 20-tall strip below, each holding two of the four
remaining 9x9 tiles.
  - In the 16-tall strip, those two tiles are always flush against one end
    or the other - starting at row 0 (the top of the whole board) or row 7
    (16-9, the bottom of that strip) - never anywhere in between. 24
    distinct x-position pairings occur, each appearing exactly 40 times
    (24 x 40 = 960) - evenly distributed, no single dominant arrangement.
  - In the taller 20-tall strip, the two tiles land at row-offsets of
    exactly 0, 5, 6, or 11 within that strip (20-9=11, so 0/11 are again
    the flush-top/flush-bottom extremes) - but 5 and 6 are also allowed,
    which are the two integer positions closest to that strip's exact
    center (5.5). Nothing else occurs. 12 distinct configurations show up,
    in two tiers: 8 at 96 solutions each and 4 at 48 each (8x96 + 4x48 =
    960).
  So the top-level finding (the line sits at one special place, not a free
  range of 35) echoes one level down: each strip the line creates shows its
  own miniature version of the same phenomenon - a small, fixed menu of
  allowed positions (always the extremes, sometimes also dead center),
  never a free continuum. The combinatorial reason behind either level
  hasn't been worked out - noted here as an observed structural fact from
  the complete dataset, not yet an explained one.


Structural finding: the "99999" outlier
-----------------------------------------
Prefix "99999" - the very first branch in the search's traversal order
(the search always tries the largest remaining tile first, so the first
five placements being 9,9,9,9,9 fills the entire top row with five 9x9
tiles) - turned out to be an extreme outlier in size. Automatic splitting
eventually broke it into 177 sub-ranges (reaching 10 digits deep), and it
produced 318,261 solutions on its own - about 18% of the entire puzzle's
solutions from a single top-level branch out of many thousands.


Darryl's serif-I conjecture
------------------------------
Proposed shape: a column of five 9x9 tiles centered in the frame (columns
18-26, full height), plus one more 9x9 tile as a "serif" beside each end
of the column (using all nine 9x9 tiles between the column and the four
serifs). Confirmed NOT completable with the remaining tiles (sizes 1-8) -
proven by exhaustive search of the remaining 1296-cell region (119,208
nodes explored, every branch tried and failed), not just "not found in
the time available."
